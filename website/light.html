An essay in seven letters

Light, unfolded

Almost everything we know about the world beyond our hands reached us as light. The blue of the sky, the colour of a star, the green of a leaf: each is a message carried by a wave, and each message was changed by what the wave met on its way. This essay is about those changes. The name of this project, read one letter at a time, gives seven of them.

Diogo Poeta

E L Y ν R I S

E

The electric field

Light is a field that trembles. At every point of space there is an electric field, an arrow with a length and a direction. In a light wave that arrow swings back and forth about five hundred million million times each second. A magnetic field swings with it, at right angles, and the two carry each other forward at the speed of light.1

When the wave reaches matter, the field pushes on electrons. They are bound to their atoms, so they cannot fly away; they sway with the field instead, and a swaying charge sends out new light. Reflection, colour, resonance: everything else in this essay comes from this small exchange between a field and the electrons it touches.

In this workEvery simulation on this site solves for this field. In Part I you can see it gather at the two faces of a silver film.

L

Layers

Put a thin layer in the way of light and the wave divides. Part of it reflects from the top face, part from the bottom face, and the two parts meet again on the way out. Where crest meets crest they add; where crest meets trough they cancel. Which colours survive depends on the thickness of the layer, counted in wavelengths. This is why a soap film shows colours, and why the coating on a camera lens can make a reflection almost vanish.1

Each material answers light with two numbers. The refractive index n says how much the wave slows and bends inside it; the extinction coefficient k says how quickly it fades. A metal has a large k. A silver film thirty nanometres thick, a little over a hundred layers of atoms, already turns back almost all near-infrared light.2

In this workThe TiO₂ coatings of Part III change colour with their thickness. For more on the two numbers, read n and k, explained.

Y

The Yee cell

In 1966 Kane Yee found a way to let a computer follow light step by step.3 He divided space into small boxes and placed the electric field on the edges of each box and the magnetic field on its faces, half a box apart. Each field is then renewed from the turning of the other, half a time step later, and on the grid the wave walks forward as it does in space. The method is called finite-difference time-domain, FDTD.

It is honest in a useful way. The computer knows nothing of lenses or plasmons, only Maxwell’s equations and the grid. When the grid is too coarse the answer is wrong, and the only way to know is to make the grid finer and watch whether the answer stops changing. For the silver film of Part I, a grid step of one nanometre agreed with the exact formula to a tenth of a percent on average; a step of five nanometres missed it by almost two percent.

In this workMeep, a free FDTD solver, is used throughout.4 Part I checks it against a second method that needs no grid at all.

ν

Frequency

The colour of light is its frequency, written with the Greek letter ν. Frequency also fixes the energy of each grain of light, the photon. A photon of red light carries about 1.8 electronvolts, a violet one about 3, and in the vacuum ultraviolet, below 200 nanometres, more than 6.

A molecule holds its outermost electron with a certain energy, for most airborne organic compounds between about 8 and 12 electronvolts. A photon below that threshold does nothing, however many arrive; a photon above it can take the electron away. Each molecule has its own threshold, so a light whose frequency can be turned, step by step, can ask a gas which of its molecules let go, and in that way tell them apart. Few lasers reach that far into the ultraviolet, and fewer still can be tuned, so here the light is made by tripling the frequency of laser light inside a glass membrane 600 nanometres thick, thinner than the wavelength of red light.5

In this workPart II: tunable vacuum-ultraviolet light down to 120 nm, and how it picked out benzene in a mixture of gases.

R

Resonance

A child on a swing goes higher when pushed in time with the swing. Electrons, and whole particles, have their own rhythms too, and when light arrives in time with one of them the response grows. A particle of titanium dioxide smaller than the wavelength can ring for light like a small bell.

Sometimes two ways of ringing cancel each other outside the particle while both stay strong inside it. The particle then almost stops scattering, it nearly disappears from view, yet the light is held within it. This state is called an anapole, “without poles”.6 In 2024 we reported anapole states excited by ultraviolet-A light, in TiO₂ nano-rectangles 190 by 160 by 100 nanometres, in simulation.7

In this workPart III, and the longer story in Anapoles, explained.

I

Interference

Whenever two waves meet they add, crest with crest, or cancel, crest with trough. Interference is behind the colours of the layers and behind the anapole. It is also behind the transparent metal of Part I. There, lines of a semiconductor above and below a silver film hand the light to waves that run along the two faces of the metal, surface plasmons. The two faces are so close that their waves join, and through them, in the calculation, about 94 percent of the near-infrared light crosses a film that alone lets through 2 percent.

Interference even explains why light is slow inside matter. The wave that moves forward in a material is the sum of the arriving wave and all the small waves sent out by the swaying electrons.1 Near the frequencies where the electrons answer strongly, that sum advances slowly, and the energy it carries travels at only a fraction of the speed of light: in ozone-grown TiO₂ films near 380 nanometres, about one fifth.8

In this workPart I, the transparent metal, and Part III, slow light in thin films.

S

Scattering

When light meets something small, it is sent off in new directions. The sky is blue because the molecules of the air scatter short wavelengths far more strongly than long ones, roughly as the inverse fourth power of the wavelength.9 Almost everything we see that does not shine by itself, we see by scattered light.

For a designer, scattering is both the tool and the test. A metasurface is a surface covered with small particles, each scattering light in a chosen way; together they can steer it, colour it, or turn it into a new frequency. Calculating and measuring what a structure scatters is how we learn whether it does what the drawing promised.

In this workStudio turns a drawing of such particles into a layout and a simulation script.

Seven letters, one wave. This project follows light carefully through small things, by calculation and by measurement, and tries to say clearly which is which.
