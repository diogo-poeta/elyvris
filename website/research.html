ENERGY TRANSPORT

Energy velocity in
dispersive thin films.

Energy velocity is the ratio of cycle-averaged Poynting flux to electromagnetic energy density. The thin-film study evaluates this quantity using measured optical constants and computed fields.

Open diagram: energy flux and stored energy

Phase, group and energy velocities

Phase velocity vp = ω / k
The velocity of a point of constant phase. It does not by itself specify the velocity of energy transport.
Group velocity vg = dω / dk
The velocity associated with a narrowband wave packet’s envelope, under conditions where loss and pulse reshaping are sufficiently small.
Energy velocity ve = ⟨S⟩ / ⟨u⟩
The ratio of cycle-averaged energy flux to stored energy density. The Poynting vector ⟨S⟩ has units of W/m² and ⟨u⟩ has units of J/m³; their ratio is a velocity vector.

These phase- and group-velocity formulas use a real propagation constant k for a transparent, homogeneous reference medium. In an absorbing medium the wavevector is complex; phase velocity is ω/Re(k), and a velocity inferred from the phase dispersion need not describe pulse or energy transport.

Transport near the absorption edge

The TiO₂ films in this work operate close to their absorption edge in the UV-A. Their refractive index changes with wavelength, and their extinction coefficient is small but finite. A slope extracted from the dispersion relation alone is therefore not a complete description of transport in the finite film.

In an absorbing band, a pulse can change shape as it propagates. Its peak need not follow the flow of energy. An unusual, negative or formally superluminal group velocity does not imply that energy or information travels faster than c.

The analysis combines computed fields with measured optical constants to evaluate local energy flux and recoverable stored energy at the wavelengths of interest.

Thesis chapter 6, sections 6.2–6.3, printed pp. 60–65 (PDF pp. 78–83). General background: Philbin, 2011.

METHOD

Calculation of energy velocity

  1. Optical constants

    Use ellipsometry-derived n(λ) and κ(λ) to describe the complex permittivity of the approximately 98 nm, ozone-grown TiO₂ film.

  2. Electromagnetic fields

    Run linear FDTD in MEEP and extract steady-state fields at each frequency for a 100 nm slab model. At each selected frequency, reproduce the measured complex permittivity.

  3. Poynting flux and energy density

    Evaluate the Poynting vector from the fields and a Brillouin-type energy density using the separately measured frequency dependence of the permittivity. Inspect local maps and the averaging region together.

Conditions for equal energy and group velocities

For a propagating plane wave in an appropriate homogeneous, transparent, lossless dispersive medium, the energy velocity and group velocity coincide. They are not universally different. The equivalence requires the relevant field, material and averaging assumptions.

The energy density also needs care: Brillouin’s familiar expression is exact for monochromatic fields in a lossless dispersive medium. This thesis uses it as an operational, recoverable-energy estimate for weakly absorbing films. Strong dissipation requires a material model that consistently separates stored and irreversibly lost energy.

RESULTS AND SCOPE

Energy velocity
and third-harmonic response.

The thesis reports normalized energy-velocity estimates of approximately 0.18–0.21c at 380–400 nm for the ozone-grown film model. These estimates describe transport at the generated third-harmonic wavelengths; the experimental pump wavelengths were 1140 and 1200 nm. They do not establish the same reduction in energy velocity at the pump. The estimates depend on the material model, geometry and spatial averaging used in the study.

A reduced flux-to-stored-energy ratio helps identify conditions worth investigating for stronger light–matter interaction. It does not alone predict third-harmonic conversion efficiency or establish an intrinsic nonlinear coefficient χ(3).

The measured reflected third harmonic comes from a coupled TiO₂/Si stack. Interference, absorption and the silicon substrate can all affect it. The thesis also notes that numerical differences between energy and group velocity can depend on the spatial averaging window.

Thesis chapter 6, sections 6.4–6.6, printed pp. 68–76 (PDF pp. 86–94).

OPTICAL ANAPOLES

Anapole excitation
in TiO₂ nanostructures.

An optical anapole is an interference condition that suppresses a leading radiation channel while internal fields remain. The FDTD field cycle and the published timing samples below describe different calculations.

Multipole interference

Light polarizes a dielectric particle, creating a spatially varying displacement-current distribution. A Cartesian multipole expansion describes parts of the resulting radiation using electric and toroidal terms.

Those two terms can feed the same electric-dipole-like far-field pattern. At the anapole condition, their amplitudes and phases suppress the combined radiation contribution while each term remains non-zero.

D = p + i k0c T = 0k0 = 2π / λ0   ·   |p| ≠ 0   ·   |T| ≠ 0

The electric anapole condition in Poeta et al. (2024), Eq. (1), using that paper’s Cartesian moment convention. Here λ0 is the vacuum wavelength and c is the speed of light. In the studied particle, a minimum of |D| identifies proximity to this ideal condition.

Identification of the anapole condition

A low scattering or transmission value can come from several mechanisms. In this research, identification involved checking the multipole contributions, their relative phase and the spatial field distributions together.

“Non-radiating” describes suppression of a selected leading channel in this driven structure. It does not establish perfect invisibility, zero absorption, or the absence of all other multipoles. Nor is it an isolated state that can hold light indefinitely.

Thesis chapter 7, sections 7.2–7.4, printed pp. 77–82 (PDF pp. 95–100); section 7.9, printed pp. 88–92 (PDF pp. 106–110).

UV-A DESIGN

Material and geometry selection

The thesis extends the design of dielectric anapoles toward UV-A wavelengths. This requires high refractive index, low extinction and nanometre-scale control of the geometry.

The study investigated amorphous TiO₂ nano-rectangles. Material dispersion and absorption vary with wavelength and affect the interference condition.

The numerical optical design targets UV photonics and nonlinear light–matter interaction, including possible third-harmonic generation. Fabrication and measurements of nonlinear enhancement were left for future work.

MY CONTRIBUTION

Numerical design
of UV-A anapoles.

I designed the TiO₂ nanostructures, ran Lumerical FDTD simulations and used MENP for the multipole decomposition that identified the UV-A anapole condition. Rafael Vilarinho and I first validated the multipole implementation against its reference examples. We contributed equally to the published study.

The thesis examines approximately 190 × 160 × 100 nm nano-rectangles under normally incident, linearly polarized light, with the electric field aligned to the long axis. It reports single-particle designs in the 350–380 nm region and a proposed periodic arrangement on fused silica near 370 nm.

The anapole condition depends on geometry, polarization, substrate and array coupling. The quoted dimensions describe the configurations studied in the thesis.
