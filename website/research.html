From transparent metals to ultraviolet light that can smell the air

Five years of photonics, told through the figures of two theses and four journal articles: how to make a metal film let light through, how to make tunable vacuum-ultraviolet light, how to use it to tell gas molecules apart, and how to design materials and nanostructures that could make it all smaller.

1 Making a metal transparent

MSc in Micro and Nano Technologies · University of Minho and International Iberian Nanotechnology Laboratory · 20211

Touch screens and solar cells need electrodes that conduct electricity and let light through. Metals conduct well, but even a film 30 nm thick reflects most of the light that reaches it. The question of this thesis was whether a pattern of another material, placed on both sides of a continuous metal film, could make the metal transparent without cutting holes in it.

The patterned material was a transition metal dichalcogenide (TMDC) such as MoS₂, a layered semiconductor with a very high refractive index. Patterned into a grating above and below the metal, it couples the incoming light to surface plasmons, oscillations of the electrons at the two faces of the metal, which carry the light across the film (Figure 1).

In simulations, the best designs transmitted up to 90% of the light through 30 nm of silver, about ten times more than the bare film. Because the effect depends on the grating, it can be tuned by changing the geometry. One variant transmits one polarisation and blocks the other, which makes it a polariser in the near infrared (Figure 2).1

From the simulation to the laboratory

The experimental part started from MoS₂ flakes exfoliated with adhesive tape and stacked as MoS₂–gold–MoS₂ around a 30 nm gold film. Under the microscope, flakes of different thickness show different colours (Figure 3c). Measured through two such samples, the stack transmitted up to about 1.5 to 1.6 times as much light as the bare gold, at near-infrared wavelengths. To pattern the flakes without a cleanroom, I cut gratings directly into them by laser ablation: lines 0.4 µm wide, 2.16 µm apart (Figures 3a and 3b).1

Possible uses named in the thesis: transparent electrodes for solar cells, plasmonic colour filters, polarisers, and resonant cavities that make photodetectors more efficient.1

2 The problem the PhD set out to solve

PhD in Physics · Bielefeld University · 20262

Indoor air carries volatile organic compounds (VOCs) from paints, cleaning products and fuels. Some, like benzene, are regulated at concentrations below one part per million. The standard portable detector, a photoionization detector, uses an ultraviolet lamp to knock an electron off each gas molecule and counts the resulting current. But the lamp emits at a fixed photon energy, so every molecule that can be ionised at that energy adds to the same signal. The detector tells you how much, not what.2

Each molecule needs a different minimum photon energy to be ionised, its ionisation potential. Most VOCs lie between about 7.75 and 11.8 eV, which corresponds to vacuum-ultraviolet (VUV) light of about 160 to 105 nm. A VUV source whose wavelength can be tuned across those thresholds would let a detector tell molecules apart.2

3 Making tunable vacuum-ultraviolet light

Vacuum-ultraviolet light is absorbed by air and by most optical materials, which is why it is hard to make and to handle. The approach here was nonlinear optics. A femtosecond laser drives an optical parametric amplifier, which produces a tunable wavelength. A crystal of β-barium borate doubles its frequency into the near ultraviolet. Finally a suspended SiO₂ membrane only 600 nm thick triples it, by third-harmonic generation, into the VUV (Figure 5). The membrane is thin enough that phase matching, the main limit of bulk crystals, matters much less.2,3

The source is tunable across 120–180 nm, with generation shown at 130 nm and at 120 nm. Coupled to a custom detector, it ionised ethanolamine and eucalyptol, and the signal grew linearly with the ethanolamine concentration.3 My part of this work was the gas-handling system, the low-noise current amplifier and the data-acquisition software, together with simulations of the third-harmonic generation and the measurements.2

4 Telling molecules apart

With a tunable source, the detector can do something a lamp cannot. As the photon energy is raised step by step, the current jumps each time it crosses the ionisation potential of a compound present in the gas. The size of each jump is proportional to that compound’s concentration, weighted by how easily it ionises. Taking the derivative of the current turns the steps into peaks, one per molecule: a spectrum of the mixture. The method is called sample ionization potential spectroscopy (SIPS).4

Applied to a certified mixture of benzene, toluene, ethylbenzene and xylenes (BTEX), it separated the individual compounds at the concentrations of occupational exposure limits: 0.5 ppm benzene, 50 ppm toluene, 20 ppm ethylbenzene and 50 ppm of each xylene (Figure 7).2,4 The experiments and measurements were carried out by my co-authors. My contribution was the earlier lamp-based photoionization detector project that this set-up grew from, the research discussion, and writing and editing the manuscript.2

5 A better material: TiO₂ films and slow light

Third-harmonic generation is weak, so the next question was how to make it stronger. One route is a material with a larger nonlinear response in a geometry that holds the light longer. TiO₂ has a high refractive index and low absorption in the near ultraviolet. It can be grown atomic layer by atomic layer by atomic layer deposition (ALD), which controls thickness to a fraction of a nanometre. Growing it with different oxidants (water, hydrogen peroxide, ozone) gave films with different structure, defects and optical constants (Figure 8).2,5

To compare films before measuring them, I needed a number that says how long light lingers in a material. The usual choice, the group velocity, can give nonsense, even faster than light, near an absorption edge. The energy velocity ve = ⟨S⟩/⟨u⟩, the energy flow divided by the stored energy, stays physical. It follows directly from the measured n(λ) and k(λ). In ozone-grown films it fell to about 0.18–0.21 of the speed of light near 380–400 nm, confirmed by ellipsometry and simulation (Figure 9).5

The third-harmonic measurements followed the material chemistry. The hydrogen-peroxide film gave the strongest signal at 400 nm, while the thicker ozone film was strongest at 380 nm (Figure 10). Because the energy velocity comes straight from ellipsometry data, it costs almost nothing to calculate and can screen film thickness, oxidant and wavelength before anything is fabricated.5

6 Nanostructures that hold the light: anapoles

The second route to stronger third-harmonic generation is to concentrate the light inside nanostructures. In simulations, TiO₂ nano-rectangles 190 × 160 × 100 nm support an anapole near 366 nm, a state in which the particle scatters very little while the field inside is strong. Arranged in a metasurface on a thin silica membrane, the effect appears at 370 nm.6 The full story, with figures and two interactive explanations, is on its own page.

7 Where this could be used

Each application below is labelled with how far the work has gone.

  • Shown in the labSelective air-quality monitoring. Telling benzene from toluene and xylenes in real time, at occupational exposure limits, for workplaces and indoor air.4
  • Shown in the labTunable VUV light from a thin membrane. A source tunable across 120–180 nm without a gas cell or a bulk crystal at the last stage.3
  • Method proposedScreening thin films before fabrication. Using the energy velocity, calculated from ellipsometry data, to choose film thickness and growth chemistry for ultraviolet nonlinear optics.5
  • SimulatedUltraviolet metasurfaces. Anapole metasurfaces in TiO₂ as compact elements for third-harmonic generation; fabrication is future work.6
  • Simulated, partly measuredTransparent metal electrodes, filters and polarisers. TMDC-patterned metal films for solar cells and photodetectors.1

The long-term goal joins these threads: a compact, tunable ultraviolet gas sensor that names the molecules it detects.2
