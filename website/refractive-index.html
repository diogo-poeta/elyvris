n and k: the two numbers behind every optical simulation

Tables of refractive indices are easy to find. What is harder to find is what the numbers mean, and how the same two numbers decide the result of a coating design, a metasurface, a plasmonic antenna or a photonic chip.

Diogo Poeta · about 8 minutes

1. What they mean 2. Explore real materials 3. At a surface 4. Why they come together 5. In simulations 6. Same name, different numbers

1 One complex number per wavelength

Inside a material, light behaves as if travelling through a medium with a complex refractive index

ñ = n + i k

The real part n, the refractive index, tells how much the wave slows down. Its speed becomes c/n and its wavelength shrinks from λ to λ/n. The imaginary part k, the extinction coefficient, tells how fast it is absorbed. The intensity falls by a factor e ≈ 2.7 over a depth

δ = λ / (4πk)

Both depend on the wavelength. A material is never described by “its” refractive index, only by n(λ) and k(λ) over a stated range.1

3 What happens at a surface

Where light meets a material, part of it is reflected. At normal incidence from air the reflected fraction is

R = [(n − 1)² + k²] / [(n + 1)² + k²]

Glass, with n ≈ 1.46, reflects about 3.5% per surface. Crystalline silicon at 633 nm (n ≈ 3.87) reflects about 35%. Gold at 633 nm has a small n (0.18) and a large k (3.4), and reflects about 94%: a large k makes a mirror, not a black surface, as long as n is small.3,4

Physicists often use the permittivity ε = ñ² instead, which is what Maxwell’s equations take directly. Its real part ε₁ = n² − k² becomes negative when k > n. That is the signature of a metal at optical frequencies: gold at 633 nm has ε₁ ≈ −12. A negative ε₁ is what allows surface plasmons, the collective oscillations of free electrons at a metal surface.5

4 Why n and k come together

A material’s response cannot come before the light that causes it. This causality links n and k through the Kramers–Kronig relations: if you know k at all wavelengths you can calculate n, and the other way round.1 One practical consequence is that n changes fastest, and usually peaks, near a wavelength where the material starts to absorb. TiO₂ shows this clearly: as k rises below about 350 nm, n climbs to its maximum (Figure 3a on the anapole page). This is why the most useful high-index materials are often used just on the long-wavelength side of their absorption edge, where n is high and k is still small.

Another consequence: a material model with a constant n and zero k over a wide band is an approximation that only holds far from any absorption. It is fine for glass in the visible. It is not fine for TiO₂ in the ultraviolet.

6 Same name, different numbers

“TiO₂” is not one set of optical constants. At 366.5 nm, two published data sets for TiO₂ films grown by atomic layer deposition give n = 2.87 (Siefke et al.) and n = 2.81 (Jolivet et al.).12,13 Crystal phase, deposition temperature, oxidant and thickness all change the result. In our own ALD films, the choice of oxidant changed the optical constants and the third-harmonic response.10 The difference matters: for a resonance whose wavelength scales roughly with n, a 2% change in n moves it by about 2%, some 8 nm at 370 nm (a rough estimate).

Before using a table of n and k

  1. Find the sample behind it. Bulk crystal, thin film, deposition method, temperature. Use the data closest to what you will actually make.
  2. Stay inside the measured range. Extrapolating optical constants, especially towards an absorption edge, is guessing.
  3. Check whether k was measured. Some records give n only. The fused silica data used here, for example, list no k; zero is an assumption.
  4. Check the sign convention. Physics texts usually write n + ik with time dependence exp(−iωt); engineering texts often write n − ik with exp(+iωt). Mixing them turns absorption into gain.
  5. Check the units. Nanometres, micrometres and electronvolts all appear in data files.
  6. Measure when it matters. For a design that depends on a few nanometres, ellipsometry on your own film beats any table.11

You do not need another database. The refractiveindex.info collection is open and well documented.2 This page explains how to read it. To see what one layer does to a spectrum, use the single-film calculator.
