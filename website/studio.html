A finite cylinder or a box has no exact formula for light scattering. Its spectrum has to be computed with a numerical solver (for example the finite-difference time-domain method, FDTD), which this page does not include. Showing a curve here would mean inventing it.

What you can do now: inspect the shape in 3D and download a gdsfactory script that draws its outline as a layout file (GDS), which opens in KLayout. A layout is a drawing for fabrication; it does not calculate light.

For a first estimate of where resonances appear, switch to a sphere or a 2D cylinder of similar size and material.

What is calculated, in plain words

The picture

A plane wave of light travels through a uniform, transparent medium and meets one particle. Part of the light is scattered (sent in other directions) and part is absorbed (turned into heat). Together they are the extinction: extinction = scattering + absorption.

The numbers

An efficiency Q is the cross section divided by the particle’s geometric shadow, so it has no unit. For a sphere the shadow is πr². For a 2D cylinder it is the width 2r, per unit length. Q = 2 means the particle removes twice the light that hits its shadow. Lengths are in nanometres (nm).

Resonances

At some wavelengths the field inside the particle builds up and scattering peaks. The second plot splits the scattering into terms of the exact series. For a sphere the first terms are the electric dipole, magnetic dipole, electric quadrupole and magnetic quadrupole.

Method, assumptions and checks

Method

Sphere: Lorenz–Mie theory. Cylinder: the corresponding exact series for an infinitely long circular cylinder with light arriving perpendicular to its axis. Both follow Bohren and Huffman, Absorption and Scattering of Light by Small Particles (Wiley, 1983) ↗, chapters 4 and 8. The original sphere solution is G. Mie, Ann. Phys. 330, 377–445 (1908) ↗.

The size parameter is x = 2π nmedium r / λ and the relative index is m = (n + ik) / nmedium, with k ≥ 0 for an absorbing material. λ is the wavelength in vacuum.

The names of the sphere’s terms (a₁ electric dipole, b₁ magnetic dipole, a₂ electric quadrupole, b₂ magnetic quadrupole) are standard; for their role in high-index particles such as silicon see A. I. Kuznetsov et al., Science 354, aag2472 (2016) ↗. For the cylinder the plot shows the angular orders n = 0, 1, 2, 3 of the series without multipole names.

Assumptions

  • One isolated particle. No substrate, no neighbouring particles, no coupling.
  • Perfect shape, smooth surface, homogeneous and isotropic material, linear response, non-magnetic.
  • The surrounding medium is transparent and has one constant index at all wavelengths.
  • The light is a plane wave. A focused beam can excite the resonances differently.
  • The 2D cylinder is infinitely long. A real nanowire or pillar of finite length behaves differently, especially when it is short.
  • Optical constants describe the sample measured in the cited source. Your material can differ. Values are interpolated linearly and never extrapolated.
  • Each spectrum has 480 evenly spaced wavelengths. A very narrow peak, as in a large particle, can fall between two points; shorten the range to resolve it.

Checks

The browser code passes 213 automatic checks. Most of them compare it with an independent calculation that uses SciPy Bessel functions and the textbook formulas directly; total efficiencies agree to better than 1 part in 10⁷. It also reproduces the value printed in appendix A of Bohren and Huffman (Qext = 3.10543), conserves energy for lossless materials, and matches the Rayleigh limit for very small spheres. These are checks of the mathematics. They are not a comparison with measurements.

The assistant

The assistant is a rule-based reader that runs on this page. It recognises shapes, material names and formulas, sizes and units, and asks for what is missing. It is not a large language model and it cannot hold a general conversation. Connecting a language model is planned as a later, optional step.
